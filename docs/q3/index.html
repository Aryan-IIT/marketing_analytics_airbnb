Buying a property to run as an Airbnb business in Amsterdam and LA is not weak economics — it is arithmetically blocked

This page is arithmetic, not a measurement. Property prices, nightly caps and the payback horizon come from outside the listings file, so they are controls rather than assertions. Move them and watch what does and does not change.

Each bar is the occupancy a purchased unit must sustain to repay its own price over the payback horizon, at that market's median entire-home nightly rate. The rule is the nights the law allows, expressed as a share of the year; the magenta section of a bar is the part of the requirement the law forbids.

The deck could fit two bars here. With all four, the picture changes: Amsterdam is not the only blocked market. LA needs roughly 60% occupancy against an ordinance whose base cap permits 33%.

Change the nightly caps

LA's 120 nights is the Home-Sharing Ordinance base cap; Extended Home-Sharing approval can lift it. Amsterdam's 30 nights applies to unhosted short-stay rental. Clear a field to model no cap at all. LA's breakeven needs a cap above roughly 220 nights a year before it clears — the conclusion survives the whole plausible range.

The same arithmetic without the payback horizon: a full year of nights at the median rate, as a share of what the property cost. It is a ceiling, not a return — nobody lets 365 nights — and it is here only to compare markets on one scale.

Rio leads on this measure and is the one market where the capital case is not blocked by a nights cap. It is also the market whose property-price assumption we are least able to source; see Method before leaning on it.

Implication The host-perspective recommendation is not "buy in the cheapest market". In two of four markets the purchase case fails on the regulation before it ever reaches the spreadsheet, and no assumption in the ranges above rescues it.
