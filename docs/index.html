Where the Airbnb money actually works

The deck argues the case across four markets. This page lets you test it. The capital arithmetic behind Q3 and Q4 runs live in your browser, on assumptions you can move — and the last section answers for one neighbourhood and room type rather than a market median.

Antwerp · Amsterdam · Los Angeles · Rio de Janeiro  |  90,161 listings, Inside Airbnb snapshots dated 6 July 2023  |  Full deck (PDF)

What the deck established, and what this page adds

The deck answers four questions in the order a host actually asks them: where do I fit, how do I configure the listing, does the money work, and what could take it away. Its findings stand on their own and are not restated here.

Three things a slide could not do. First, show all four markets on the capital questions — the deck had room for two bars each, and the missing ones change the conclusion: two of the four markets are arithmetically blocked, not one. Second, let you move the assumptions. Q3 and Q4 are scenario arithmetic on numbers from outside the listings file. On paper that is a claim you must take on trust; here you can try to break it. Third, answer for one listing. A host has a neighbourhood and a room type, not a market median.

Master question 1 · Where do I fit

Guessing the citywide price costs a Rio host 43% and an Amsterdam host 11% — the same shortcut, four times the penalty

Rio has 156 neighbourhoods in the file and the widest gaps between them. Amsterdam has 22 and is comparatively flat, so a citywide number is nearly harmless there and badly wrong in Rio.

This is the cheapest finding on the page to act on: it costs a host nothing to look up their own neighbourhood, and in Rio it is worth 43% of the asking price.

Even after fixing neighbourhood and room type, Rio listings still spread across roughly twice the range of Amsterdam's. In Rio the middle half of a niche runs 3.1× from the 25th to the 75th percentile; in Amsterdam, 1.7×.

That spread is the reason the lookup at the bottom of this page reports a band rather than a price. Two listings that look identical in this dataset are not priced alike, and no number here can tell you which one you are.

Entire homes dominate all four markets. LA is the most room-diverse — a third of its listings are private rooms and it has the only meaningful shared-room niche.

Antwerp holds the most concentrated ownership of the four: an HHI of 22 against 1–2 for the others, and a third of its listings held by hosts with three or more.

Implication Pricing research pays off in proportion to how varied the market is. In Rio it is the single highest-return hour a host can spend; in Amsterdam the citywide number is close enough to start from.

Master question 2 · How do I configure it

Pricing like a professional earns +19% in Antwerp and costs −16% in Rio — the same instinct, opposite results

In Antwerp and Amsterdam, multi-listing hosts charge more for the same kind of unit in the same neighbourhood. In LA it is a wash. In Rio they charge visibly less.

The sign flip is the finding. "Price like the pros" is not portable advice — in Rio the professional operators are competing on volume, and copying their pricing hands away 16%.

The one lever that behaves the same way everywhere. Letting the whole place is worth 1.7× to 2.7× a private room, in every market, in nearly every neighbourhood.

Longer minimum stays cost bookings everywhere too — the correlation between minimum nights and bookings per month is negative in all four markets, steepest in LA at −0.21.

This one runs backwards, and it matters that we say so. Listings with more reviews charge less, steeply so in Rio. The plain reading — that experience earns a discount — is almost certainly wrong.

A cheap listing books faster and accumulates reviews faster. Review count is partly a record of having been cheap. Read it as a symptom of the price, not a cause of it, and do not price off this chart.

Implication Two of the four levers are universal — let the whole place, keep minimum stays short. The third, pricing posture, has to be decided market by market, and the fourth is not a lever at all.

Master question 3 · Does the money work live

Buying a property to run as an Airbnb business in Amsterdam and LA is not weak economics — it is arithmetically blocked

Each bar is the occupancy a purchased unit must sustain to repay its own price over the payback horizon, at that market's median entire-home nightly rate. The magenta rule is the nights the law allows, expressed as a share of the year.

The deck could fit two bars here. With all four, the picture changes: Amsterdam is not the only blocked market. LA needs roughly 60% occupancy against an ordinance whose base cap permits 33%.

Property prices are working assumptions, not measurements. Move the slider above and watch what does and does not change.

Change the nightly caps

LA's 120 nights is the Home-Sharing Ordinance base cap; Extended Home-Sharing approval can lift it. Amsterdam's 30 nights applies to unhosted short-stay rental. Set a market to blank to model no cap.

The same arithmetic without the payback horizon: a full year of nights at the median rate, as a share of what the property cost. It is a ceiling, not a return — nobody lets 365 nights — and it is here only to compare markets on one scale.

Rio leads on this measure and is the one market where the capital case is not blocked by a nights cap. It is also the market whose property-price assumption we are least able to source; see Method before leaning on it.

Implication The host-perspective recommendation is not "buy in the cheapest market". In two of four markets the purchase case fails on the regulation before it ever reaches the spreadsheet, and no assumption in the ranges above rescues it.

Master question 4 · What could take it away live

The same fifteen nights a month is 3.6× minimum wage in Rio and 0.56× in Antwerp — one is an investment, the other is pocket money

Gross monthly takings at the local median entire-home rate, divided by that country's statutory monthly minimum wage. Everything stays in local currency — no FX is involved in this comparison, which is the point of using a wage as the yardstick.

Below 1.0× the listing is supplementary income. Rio sits far above it, which is why Rio attracts investor conversion — and the regulatory attention that follows it.

The places regulators moved on are the places that had professionalised fastest — roughly double the citywide rate in Amsterdam Centrum, half again in Rio's Zona Sul.

For a host, the income multiple and the regulatory risk are the same number read twice. The markets worth converting a property in are the markets where conversion is being legislated against.

Implication Rio's income multiple is the strongest result in the analysis and the most fragile. It is strong enough to attract capital, which is exactly what triggers the rule changes that would remove it.

Method and sources

The data

Four Inside Airbnb summary files, all dated 6 July 2023: 90,161 listings after cleaning. The filenames are misleading and worth stating plainly — listings-Belgium is Antwerp only, listings-Netherlands is Amsterdam's 22 districts, and listings_California is Los Angeles County.

Cleaning

Listings priced at zero or below are dropped (19 across all four files). Blank reviews_per_month is filled with 0 rather than dropped — it is blank exactly when the review count is zero, so it is structurally missing, and dropping those rows would delete 42% of Rio. minimum_nights is capped at 365. A host is "professional" at three or more listings. These rules live in findings.py and the build script applies them once.

What this data cannot answer

There are no bookings in this file. Reviews per month is a proxy for booking activity and a biased one — guests review at different rates in different markets. There is no occupancy, no revenue, no cost, and no time series, so nothing here is a forecast. The Q3 and Q4 sections are explicitly arithmetic on stated assumptions, which is why they are adjustable rather than asserted.

How to reproduce this page

Download the four Inside Airbnb files into datasets/ and run dashboard/build/export_assets.py. It rewrites every JSON file and every figure in docs/, and prints a twenty-number regression table it must reproduce exactly before the output is trustworthy. Nothing on this page is typed in by hand except the prose.

Two numbers we would not defend as measured

Rio's property price per m² and LA's nightly cap are the two inputs carrying the most weight relative to how well we can source them, and both are marked below. Rio's price drives its "strongest capital case" result; LA's cap is the difference between "tight" and "blocked". Move both and see how much of the conclusion survives — for LA, all of it does until the cap passes roughly 220 nights a year.

What we changed from the deck

Nothing in the arithmetic. The four missing bars on Q3 and Q4 are new, the per-cell lookup is new, and the accent colour was sampled from the deck file rather than estimated. Every shared number reproduces.

Numbers from outside the listings file

Rendered from the build's own record, so this page cannot claim a source the build did not write down. Rows marked in magenta are the ones we could not independently confirm.

Currency Prices are never compared across markets in their raw form. Within-market work uses local currency throughout; the only cross-market money comparison is Q4's, which uses each country's own minimum wage as the denominator and so needs no exchange rate at all.
