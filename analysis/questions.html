Twenty candidate questions, tested

MS 491 Marketing Analytics · Airbnb group assignment · four cities: Antwerp, Amsterdam, Rio de Janeiro, Los Angeles County

This is a shortlist to argue over, not a report. Every question below was run against all four cities before it was written down. Anything that did not survive the test is in the last section with the reason. We need to pick eight: three from the traveller, three from the regional manager, two from the host. Questions are ranked within each perspective, strongest first, so the top three in each block are the default answer unless someone makes a better case.

What "tested" means here. Every number comes from the standard cleaning rules in findings.py: drop the 19 listings priced at zero, fill blank reviews_per_month with 0 rather than dropping the row (that field is blank exactly when number_of_reviews is 0, so dropping would delete 42% of Rio), cap minimum_nights at 365, and ignore last_review as the brief instructs.

Currency. Prices are EUR in Antwerp and Amsterdam, BRL in Rio, USD in LA. No question compares raw prices. The default normalisation is the price index — each listing's price divided by its own city's median — which is unit-free and needs no exchange rate. Where a question needs an actual money comparison, that is stated explicitly and FX and PPP are both reported, because they disagree (question P2.3).

The control that matters. Most comparisons are made within neighbourhood and room type, then summarised across cells. This is not fussiness. Pooled comparisons reverse the sign of the professional-host result in Rio and hide it entirely in Amsterdam — a Simpson's paradox we already hit once. Where a result is reported pooled, it is because the within-cell version was checked and agreed.

P1 — The traveller

Someone deciding where to go and what to book. The useful questions here are the ones that change what they actually do: which city, which tier, which neighbourhood, and what they are risking.

Why someone asks it. This is the first real decision a traveller makes, and it is not about price, it is about privacy. "Can I afford a door I can close?"

The cities split two-to-one-to-one. In Amsterdam a below-median budget already puts you mostly among whole apartments; you cross the 50% line at the 28th percentile. Why someone asks it. Reviews are the only quality signal in this dataset, and a traveller wants to know their chance of drawing a blank.
Columns. number_of_reviews, price. Normalisation: none needed — the price tiers are within-city quartiles, and the output is a percentage.

The headline is Rio's top quartile: seven out of ten of the most expensive listings in the city have never been reviewed once. No — and in Rio, emphatically not. Rio's busiest quartile is priced 32% below its neighbours in every single one of 47 cells; there is not one exception. Amsterdam is nearly neutral at 0.96× with the busiest being dearer in a third of cells. Four cities, four completely different answers, which is exactly what we want from a comparative question. Antwerp has no listings near Brussels Airport, its actual international gateway 34km away — the question is unanswerable there, and that is itself worth saying out loud about a file labelled "Belgium". Amsterdam is so compact that Schiphol is within 10km of nearly half the city, so "near the airport" is not a distinct segment at all. Rio's airport zone is a genuine budget-transit market: half the city median price, 57% of it bookable for a single night against 28% citywide, but with a median of zero reviews per month, so the supply is there and dead. Trap, and read this before you pick it. We first tested airport proximity the standard way — as a price gradient across the whole market — and it fails. Within neighbourhood and room type the correlation between distance-to-airport and log price is +0.002, +0.021, −0.019 and +0.007 in the four cities: nothing, four times over. It only works as a conditional question, asked about the catchment as a segment. If anyone presents this as "listings get cheaper the further you are from the airport", the data does not support it and the slide will not survive a question. Frame it as "who is served near the airport", never as a gradient.

P2 — The Airbnb regional manager

Someone allocating marketing budget across regions and deciding where the platform should push. The useful questions are the ones that identify a problem worth spending money on, and distinguish between "no supply", "supply nobody wants" and "supply that cannot legally be sold".

P2.1Which city's supply is not converting into bookings — and why not?

Why someone asks it. The core budget question. Adding listings to a market that already cannot sell what it has is wasted money; the fix depends on which kind of failure it is.
Columns. availability_365, reviews_per_month. Normalisation: none — every figure is a share of that city's own listings.

Share of listingsAntwerpAmsterdamRioLA
Live — bookable and earning51.1%33.4%44.1%61.1%
On the shelf — bookable, never booked11.9%4.6%24.5%15.5%
Blocked — earning, but no calendar open30.6%54.3%13.9%17.4%
Dead — no calendar, no bookings6.4%7.7%17.4%6.0%

This is the strongest platform-side question we have, because the four cities land in four different quadrants and each implies a different intervention. Amsterdam's problem is not demand — it is that 54.3% of its listings are earning reviews with a completely closed calendar, which is what a 30-night-per-year cap looks like in data. Advertising into Amsterdam buys nothing; the constraint is regulatory supply, not awareness. Rio is the opposite failure: 24.5% of its stock is sitting bookable and has never sold, plus another 17.4% that is neither bookable nor selling. Rio has too much supply, not too little. LA is the healthiest market at 61.1% live, and Antwerp is middling. The one-line answer to "should I put budget behind Rio instead of Belgium" is no — Rio's problem is demand generation, and Antwerp's is that it is small.

Trap. "Blocked" mixes two different things we cannot separate: a listing sold out and a listing whose host has closed the calendar. Both produce availability_365 = 0. In Amsterdam the regulatory reading is well supported because the blocked listings still earn reviews; do not make the same claim for Antwerp without more care.

Antwerp and Amsterdam are internally efficient — every neighbourhood is within roughly a factor of two of balance, so there is no obvious place to point a budget. Rio and LA are wildly unbalanced. P2.3"Belgium looks expensive — should I put the budget behind Rio instead?" Which of these markets is actually cheap?

Why someone asks it. Because it is the question that gets asked in the meeting, and because the honest answer is that it depends entirely on a choice most people make without noticing.
Columns. price, room_type. Normalisation: this is the one question that is about normalisation, so it reports all three — price index, market FX, and PPP — and shows they disagree.

Median nightly priceAntwerpAmsterdamRioLA
In local currency€70€130R$300$109
Converted at market FX$76$142$61$109

Rio moves from cheapest to third-cheapest depending on which conversion you use, and Antwerp and Rio swap ends of the table. Neither conversion is wrong; they answer different questions. FX answers "what does this cost a foreign visitor arriving with dollars", which is the right lens for inbound tourism marketing. PPP answers "what does this cost relative to local incomes", which is the right lens for host recruitment and for judging whether a market is expensive by its own standards. A manager deciding where to advertise to international travellers should use FX and conclude Rio is cheap. A manager deciding where hosts will find listing attractive should use PPP and conclude Rio is not. Amsterdam is the dearest market under every normalisation, which is the only rank that is stable.

Trap. The PPP factors are approximate OECD-style figures and the FX rates are mid-2023, roughly contemporaneous with the July 2023 file dates. The rank reversal is robust to reasonable changes in either; the exact dollar figures are not. Present the reversal, and be upfront that the rates are assumptions we brought in from outside the data. This is also the question that justifies why every other question in the deck uses a price index instead.

Amsterdam is a genuine outlier and it reverses the pattern of the other three. Private rooms are a fifth of Amsterdam's listings and nearly half its bookings — they work more than twice as hard as their share of supply, while entire homes work at 0.63. In Antwerp the two formats are in equilibrium, and in Rio and LA entire homes do the heavier lifting. That does not invalidate the recommendation but it does change the reason, and someone will ask. Have the answer ready. It also connects to P2.1: Amsterdam's 54.3% "blocked" share is the same phenomenon seen from another angle.

P3 — The prospective host

Someone who owns a property and is deciding whether and how to list it. The useful questions produce a number they can act on this week, and an honest statement of how much the data actually pins that number down.

The honest answer differs sharply by city and it is a genuinely useful thing to tell a host. Hosts with no track record price above established neighbours everywhere, which is already counterintuitive — the entrant charges a premium rather than a discount. But the size of the mistake varies by a factor of ten. In Amsterdam the newcomer premium is a mild +9%, and Amsterdam also has the lowest never-reviewed share; in Rio it is +108%, and restricting to entire homes makes it worse at +173%. Trap. The direction of causation is genuinely ambiguous and you should say so. These listings may be unbooked because they are overpriced, or priced high because the host has no feedback yet and is guessing, or they may be a different and genuinely more expensive kind of property that simply arrived recently. The result survives winsorising at 1%/99% and holds within entire homes only, so it is not an outlier artifact — but it is an association, not proof.

Why someone asks it. Opening the calendar is the cheapest lever a host has — it costs nothing and takes a minute. Hosts assume it works.

The answer reverses sign across the four cities, which makes it one of the better comparative questions in the set. In Antwerp and Amsterdam more availability is weakly associated with more bookings, as a host would expect. In Rio it is negative in 35 of 38 cells, and in LA negative in 77% of them. The most plausible reading is that the arrow runs the other way: a listing with 300 open days is not aggressively available, it is unsold, while a listing with 40 open days has had the rest of the year booked out from under it. Trap. This is a reverse-causation question and the honest presentation says so explicitly rather than claiming availability harms bookings. The clean version of the claim is: in Rio and LA, availability cannot be used as a lever because it is mostly measuring the outcome, not the input. That is a limitation of the data, and stating it clearly is worth more than pretending the correlation is causal.

P3.4Is a crowded neighbourhood bad news for me, or is that where the guests are?

Three cities say competition is a good sign and one says the opposite. In Antwerp, Amsterdam and especially Rio, listings in bigger neighbourhoods get more bookings each, not fewer — supply has gone where the guests are and the crowd is a signal to follow. LA alone is negative: its big neighbourhoods are big because LA County is enormous and residential, not because they are tourist destinations, so scale there dilutes rather than concentrates. Amsterdam adds a second twist — its busy neighbourhoods are also its expensive ones (+0.497), so there you pay for the crowd, while in Rio the busy neighbourhoods are slightly cheaper (−0.137) and a host gets volume without paying for location.

Trap. This is neighbourhood-level, so n is small — 14 neighbourhoods in Antwerp, and correlations on 14 points are fragile. Quote Rio and LA with confidence and Antwerp with care. It is also the constructive counterpart to our double jeopardy test, which failed: treating neighbourhoods as brands does not reproduce the Ehrenberg pattern, and we report that failure deliberately.

Two things vary and they vary independently. First, how common round pricing is: 43.5% of Amsterdam listings end in a zero against 12.5% in Rio, which is largely a denomination effect — a round number in euros is a coarser instrument than a round number in reais, where the median price is 300. Second, and more interesting, whether it is associated with anything. In LA round-priced listings are meaningfully less booked in 92% of cells; in Rio they are slightly more booked in 65% of cells. Trap. The obvious confound is that round pricing is a proxy for an inattentive host, and inattentive hosts do worse for many reasons that have nothing to do with the digit. We checked whether it is simply professionals versus amateurs and it is not a clean split — in LA round-priced listings are 30.4% professional against a 39.2% base, but in Rio the shares are nearly identical (27.2% against 26.8%). So the confound is present but does not explain the reversal. Rank this last of the P3 questions: it is the most novel and the least certain, and it should only make the final eight if the team wants one genuinely unexpected slide.

Include it only if the team wants a question that gives a host a clear, confident, immediately actionable instruction — which it does, and which none of the others quite do. Note also that the causal direction is defensible here in a way it is not for P3.3: a host sets minimum nights before guests respond to it.

What we tested and killed

Everything here was actually run against all four cities. We are listing it because a failed test is harder to fabricate than a confirmed one, and because two of these are the analyses a group would reach for first.

Distance from the city centre. The standard Airbnb analysis, and it is simply wrong on this data. The correlation with log price is positive in Rio (+0.15 — the value is the beach, not Centro) and flat in LA (+0.08 — LA is polycentric and Malibu is 45km out). Antwerp has exactly one listing beyond 10km, so there is no gradient to measure. Do not let this back into the deck.

Distance to the nearest major airport, as a price gradient. The idea we were asked to evaluate. Tested against ANR and Brussels for Antwerp, Schiphol, Galeão and Santos Dumont, and LAX, Burbank, Long Beach and John Wayne. Pooled, the correlations with log price are +0.117, −0.019, +0.167 and −0.045 — weak and inconsistent in sign. Within neighbourhood and room type they collapse to +0.002, +0.021, −0.019 and +0.007: nothing at all, in all four cities. There is also no variation to exploit in the European cities — 80% of Antwerp's listings sit between 3.0 and 5.6km from its airport, and Amsterdam's between 8.0 and 13.3km. The gradient version is dead. What survives is the conditional version, P1.7 — the airport catchment treated as a segment with its own price level, stay length and demand — and that one is genuinely strong because the four cities differ completely. Do not confuse the two; the distinction is the whole finding.

Distance to the supply centroid, our attempt to rescue a geography variable by defining "the centre" from where the listings actually are rather than from a map. Within neighbourhood and room type the correlations are −0.096, −0.052, −0.032 and +0.011 — weak, and null in LA. Not worth a slide. Geography in this dataset works through named neighbourhoods (P1.6, P2.2), not through radius.

Total review count as a demand measure. It mostly measures how long a listing has existed. Dividing reviews by reviews-per-month gives an implied months-live that ranges from about 5 to about 65 months within every city, and log reviews correlates +0.44 to +0.54 with that implied age. Use reviews_per_month, a flow, wherever demand is meant.

Estimated revenue per listing. Requires an assumed review rate and an assumed stay length, neither of which is in the data. Any number produced this way is an assumption wearing a result's clothing.

Top-10 host share. A market-size artifact — it falls automatically as a market gets bigger. Share of listings from hosts with 3+ listings is the honest version and is used in P2.5.

Shared rooms and hotel rooms as segments. Too small to compare. Shared rooms are 0.3% of Amsterdam and 3.5% of LA; hotel rooms are 0.4% to 1.2% everywhere. Antwerp has 33 shared and 15 hotel listings in total, and zero neighbourhoods with 50 or more of either. Any four-city comparison would be reading noise in two of the four cities.

Neighbourhood level 1 (neighbourhood_group). Empty in three of the four files. It has three values in LA and nothing anywhere else, so it cannot support a comparative question.

Listing title in English versus the local language. A genuinely interesting positioning question that we wanted to work, and it half does. Rio is the only city with a real split at 42.7% English titles, and there the effect on bookings is exactly nothing — a rpm gap of +0.000, higher in 46% of 76 cells. Amsterdam (+0.050) and LA (+0.130) lean positive but are 94% and 86% English already. Antwerp is untestable: 85.6% English and only four mixed cells. A question we can only answer in three cities, with a null in the one city where it matters, is not worth one of eight slots — but the Rio null is a nice throwaway line if someone asks about positioning.

Keyword premia in listing titles. Tested 17 keywords within neighbourhood and room type. Only one signal is consistent across cities: "studio" in the title is a discount everywhere (0.52× to 0.79×), which is unsurprising since it describes the property, not the marketing. Everything else is thin — "view" ranges from 1.03× in Amsterdam to 1.41× in LA on 14 to 34 cells, and "beach" in Rio comes out at 0.90×, below neutral. Too few observations per cell to defend.

Double jeopardy, treating neighbourhoods as brands. Retained deliberately as a reported failure rather than dropped. The correlation between neighbourhood size and median reviews per month is +0.44, −0.09, +0.65 and −0.21 — no consistent pattern, so the law does not hold here. It is worth one slide precisely because it did not work, and P3.4 is the constructive question built on the wreckage.

The dashboard

Recommendation: build it for the host (P3), driven by P3.1, P3.2 and P3.6.

The test is which perspective is naturally parametric — where changing an input produces a genuinely different answer rather than a filtered subset of the same one. Only the host passes cleanly. A host has a specific property in a specific neighbourhood at a specific format, and every one of those choices moves the answer. The regional manager's questions are a fixed comparison of four markets; there is one right answer per city and it belongs on a slide, not behind a control. The traveller's questions are parametric too, but a traveller dashboard collapses into exactly the filter-and-histogram we should avoid: set a budget, see the listings that match. That is a search box, and Airbnb already has one.

The host case is different because the output is not a subset of listings, it is a decision with a stated confidence. That is the thing a static slide cannot do, because the confidence depends on inputs we cannot enumerate in advance.

Inputs. City; neighbourhood; room type; intended minimum nights; and a candidate nightly price the host types in.

Outputs. Four, in this order:

  • Where that price sits in the distribution of genuine comparables — same neighbourhood, same room type — as a percentile, with the median and quartiles shown. The obvious part, and the part every dashboard does.
  • How much that comparison is worth. The residual spread from P3.1: "in this neighbourhood and format, otherwise-identical listings still differ by 2.6×, so treat the median as a starting point, not an answer." This number changes with the inputs and it is the reason the dashboard has to be interactive. It is also the output no static chart can carry, because it is a statement about the strength of its own advice.
  • The minimum-nights penalty, from P3.6: the median reviews per month for that cell at the chosen minimum stay against 1 night, so the host sees what the setting costs before they choose it.
  • The newcomer warning, from P3.2: if the host has no reviews, show what other no-review listings in that cell charge and what fraction of them have never been booked. In Rio this output is dramatic and directly actionable; in Amsterdam it is nearly silent. That the same control produces a loud answer in one city and a quiet one in another is the argument for building it.

What the user actually learns. Not "here is the price" — they learn how much the market determines their price and how much is left to them, which is different in every city and every neighbourhood, and which is invisible in any single view. A host in LA leaves knowing the comps are trustworthy. A host in Rio leaves knowing the comps are nearly useless, that the typical newcomer there is priced at double the market, and that 42% of the city's listings have never been booked. Those are opposite pieces of advice from one tool, which is the case for the tool.

Argument against the generic version. A filter-and-histogram dashboard answers "what is in the data". This one answers "what should I do, and how much should I trust it". The distinguishing feature is that it reports its own uncertainty as a first-class output rather than hiding it, which is also the learning outcome the course is assessing. If we build a price slider and a bar chart, we have built something fifteen other groups could build from the same file.

Picking the eight

Default recommendation, if nobody objects: P1.1, P1.3, P1.4 for the traveller — the budget ladder, the odds of a listing with no track record, and weekend bookability with its price reversal. P2.1, P2.2, P2.3 for the manager — the inventory-health quadrants, the neighbourhood demand-supply mismatch, and the currency question that answers "should I back Rio instead" with a rank reversal. P3.1, P3.2 for the host — what the comps are worth, and the newcomer overpricing result.

That set covers all four cities in every question, contains three sign reversals and one rank reversal, extends the validated findings in four of the eight, and leaves P1.7 and P3.5 as the two most distinctive substitutes if the team wants something less conventional. The two arguments most likely to be made against it: swapping P1.7 in for P1.4 to get the airport question in front of the class, and swapping P2.5 in for P2.3 to lead with the Simpson's paradox instead of the currency reversal. Both are defensible.
